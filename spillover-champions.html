KEVCE Insights · Global R&D firms and EMDE innovation

Spillover champions

Which of the world's top R&D investors generate the most valuable knowledge spillovers — globally, to high-income countries, to emerging and developing economies, or to a particular country? Pick the scope and the measure (spillovers, marginal or average returns, private value), the statistic (mean, total or median over the firm's innovations), how many firms to show and the minimum portfolio size. Hover a bar for the firm's details; the chart's camera icon saves a PNG.

What is shown

Firms
Companies in the EU Industrial R&D Scoreboard (the world's largest R&D investors) matched to their patent families in PATSTAT; one bar per firm, computed over its innovations (patent families, patents of invention only, filed 2013–2022) with a Watson valuation. The diamonds (optional, top axis, spillovers only) show the selected scope's spillovers as a share of the firm's global spillovers, i.e. the fraction of the knowledge flows its innovations generate that is received in the region selected; the dashed line marks the mean of that share across all firms passing the filters, not only the top ones shown. Bar width can be set proportional to the firm's number of innovations in the window, or to its logarithm, so that firms whose averages rest on large portfolios stand out; very thin bars are widened slightly so that their labels stay legible.
Scope
Where the recipients of the knowledge flows are located: anywhere ("Global"), in high-income countries, in emerging and developing economies with or without China (and India), or in one of China, India, the UK, the US or the EU. The scopes are the same as in the Innovation Strategy Explorer; "EMDE (excl. China)" is the paper's main definition.
Measure
Spillovers are the expected value of the knowledge flows an innovation generates for later innovations located in the scope, in USD million, following the Patent Rank approach of Martin, Verhoeven et al.. Marginal returns relate those spillovers to the cost of the innovation: (α+1)/cost × spillovers, where α is Watson's estimate of how strongly the innovation's private value responds to its cost; the measure is set to zero for innovations whose private value already exceeds twice their cost, since no public support would be needed to bring them about. Average returns are (private value + spillovers)/cost, the total value created per dollar of R&D cost. Both returns are shown in percent of cost. Private value is the expected private (stock-market) value of the innovation to its owner, USD million, independent of scope. Returns are missing for the few innovations without a Watson cost estimate and are then computed over the rest.
Statistic
The bar shows, over the firm's innovations in the window, the mean per innovation (the default, and what the paper's firm-level analysis uses), the total (mean × number of innovations, so it rewards large portfolios; not offered for returns, which are ratios) or the median. Spillovers are very skewed — most innovations generate none in a given region — so regional medians are often exactly zero and the median ranking is only informative for global spillovers, private value and average returns.
Caveats
Averages over few innovations are noisy, which is why the minimum portfolio size defaults to 30. Firm names are the Scoreboard's; subsidiaries are folded into the parent where the Scoreboard lists them together. Pinned firms are drawn in a darker green and are shown whatever the filters (minimum portfolio size included), appended below the ranked top-N if they do not make it; set "Firms shown" to "Only pinned firms" to compare just those.
